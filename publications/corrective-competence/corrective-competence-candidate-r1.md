# Corrective Competence

After roughly ten minutes of AI assistance, people get better immediate results and worse independent ones. In a set of randomized trials across math and reading tasks, participants who had just worked with an AI assistant solved fewer problems once the assistant disappeared, and they gave up more often. That is the part of the story that matters. The system did help while it was present. The damage showed up in what happened after.

This is where a lot of current discussion about AI collaboration still goes wrong. The field keeps asking whether the system improves output, whether people trust it too much, whether the interface should surface uncertainty better, whether the recommendation is accurate enough to deserve reliance. Those questions matter. They are not the main question. The deeper issue is whether the workflow preserves the user's corrective competence: the practical ability to notice, contest, and repair what has gone wrong once assistance becomes normal.

That capacity is easy to underrate because it hides inside successful collaboration. When the model answers quickly, the draft gets produced, the diagnosis gets nudged in the right direction, or the code compiles on the first pass, the interaction looks productive. The danger is that productivity and corrective capacity can move in opposite directions. A system can raise assisted performance while weakening the human being who is still supposed to supervise it.

The most important evidence for this is no longer just anecdotal deskilling talk. The persistence study is striking because the impairment arrives so quickly. A short exposure to an always-helpful assistant was enough to reduce later unassisted performance and make people skip more often. Persistence matters here because it sits upstream of almost everything else. A person who stops working through the problem no longer gets the repetitions that build judgment. They do not just know less. They lose the habit of staying with the difficulty long enough to catch the next error.

The same pattern appears more explicitly in work on skill formation. In a study of developers learning a new asynchronous Python library, AI assistance reduced conceptual understanding, code reading, and debugging performance. The interesting part is not simply that AI hurt learning. It is that the effect depended on how people used it. The high-performing patterns were the ones that preserved cognitive engagement by asking for explanations or conceptual help. The weak patterns were the ones that treated the model like a contractor and the user like a reviewer of finished work. That is the boundary the essay needs to name. What matters is not just access to AI help. It is whether the interaction keeps the person cognitively inside the task.

The pedagogical version of the same problem has been described as epistemic debt. In a study of novice programmers, unrestricted AI assistance and scaffolded AI produced similar short-term productivity, but they diverged sharply when the system was removed. In the subsequent maintenance task, the unrestricted condition collapsed much more often. The point is not that friction is always good. It is that some forms of friction are doing the work that competence used to do. If the workflow removes every moment in which the person has to reconstruct, explain, or debug the reasoning, it borrows against oversight that will be needed later.

This is why corrective competence is the right term for the problem. The issue is not generic understanding in the abstract. It is not literary concern about whether people are becoming shallow. It is whether the human participant still retains enough ability to intervene well when the system is partly right, impressively wrong, or wrong in a way that hides its own error. In safety engineering, this becomes even sharper. The strongest formulation in the current seam is the idea of a competence shadow: not the mistakes the AI visibly makes, but the possibilities it prevents the human from considering at all. Once the system narrows which hypotheses are generated, which scenarios are explored, and which mitigations stay alive, the resulting oversight can look intact while already being hollow.

That is also why the best rival explanation, appropriate reliance, is real but smaller. Yes, calibration matters. Yes, people can over-rely on bad advice or under-rely on good advice. A study of radiologists working with AI on prostate cancer MRI diagnosis found persistent under-reliance even when the AI outperformed the human experts, and performance feedback did not solve the problem cleanly. That is important evidence. It does not dislodge the deeper frame. It shows that the pathology is not simply "people trust too much." Human-AI collaboration can fail when people defer too easily, and it can fail when they never learn how to integrate the system's strengths without losing their own judgment. Reliance is the visible behavior. Corrective competence is the underlying capacity that determines whether reliance can ever become safe.

The expert-domain support papers make the same point from the other side. In computational pathology, researchers measure automation bias, anchoring, confirmation effects, and time-pressure amplification. AI support often improves average performance. It also creates identifiable ways in which a previously correct human judgment gets overwritten by a bad suggestion, or a first answer keeps exerting force after the evidence should have displaced it. Those are not merely calibration errors in a narrow sense. They are failures of correction under assistance. The user is still "in the loop," but the loop has been reorganized around accepting, weighting, and defending suggestions rather than generating and testing alternatives.

That distinction becomes clearer when the system is designed differently. Evaluative AI proposes a hypothesis-driven form of decision support that gives evidence for or against a candidate judgment instead of just issuing a recommendation. That matters because recommendation-driven systems ask the user to react to the machine's answer. A hypothesis-driven system is closer to what oversight actually requires. It keeps the person in a position to compare possibilities, inspect reasons, and decide what the evidence licenses. In other words, it does not merely improve the answer surface. It has a chance of preserving corrective competence.

This is the shift the field needs. The practical question is not whether the AI helps. Plenty of systems help. The practical question is what remains of the person after help becomes the default mode. If the workflow steadily weakens the human capacity to detect, challenge, and repair errors, then a higher assisted-output metric is a bad measure of readiness. It may even be evidence of a trap.

That should change how delegation gets evaluated. A workflow that is safe to delegate through should preserve some combination of independent first-pass judgment, visible evidence, challengeable outputs, and checkpoints that test whether the human can still explain or reconstruct the reasoning when the model is absent. The exact mix will vary by domain. The principle should not. Do not ask only whether the system improves the task. Ask whether the collaboration preserves enough corrective competence for the human being to matter when it counts.

## Limits

This essay does not claim that AI assistance always reduces competence, that users must independently replicate every task, or that calibration and interface design are irrelevant. It argues for a narrower point: in many important workflows, the decisive risk is not just bad advice but degraded human capacity to correct bad advice once assistance becomes habitual.
