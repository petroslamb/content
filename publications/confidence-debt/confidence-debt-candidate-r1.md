# Confidence Debt

The summary said every obligation was properly mapped. In a verification project built to test whether AI could reliably trace Ethereum protocol requirements to actual implementation code, one run family produced a polished report alongside its raw evidence. The report was structured, confident, and easy to forward. The row-level data underneath it was not. Spot-checks turned up client mappings that pointed to ABI helpers, test fixtures, and RPC glue rather than to the protocol logic the run was supposed to verify. In one model tier, thirteen of fifteen direct-adjudication entries required follow-up. The summary and the spreadsheet came from the same system, generated in the same session, delivered with the same tone of certainty. One of them was wrong about where the code actually lived. The other one was easier to read.

That is the part of this story that made it hard to put down. The model's mistakes were ordinary. Every verification tool produces errors. What was not ordinary was the realization that, without an explicit rule for deciding which artifact to believe, the polished version would have propagated. It was faster to scan, simpler to present in a meeting, and more comfortable to treat as evidence. The raw data required someone to open a spreadsheet, trace a file path back to a repository, and check whether the location contained protocol logic or peripheral glue. In any workflow that rewards readability and penalizes friction, the confident version wins and the contradictions stay buried.

The project was forced to invent its own adjudication rules after the fact. A working hierarchy: raw evidence outranks evaluation summaries, evaluation summaries outrank narrative readmes. A contradiction register, because once the same system produces incompatible claims, contradiction has to become a tracked object rather than an embarrassment. A scoring rubric that could distinguish mostly right from partially right from semantically disputed, because "looks plausible" was carrying more authority than it deserved. Eight contradictions surfaced across eighty-one scored mappings. Three public-facing claims were downgraded. The follow-up burden concentrated in one model tier, which meant model selection was doing governance work that the project had originally treated as a procurement question.

Those results are inspectable. The scored dataset, the contradiction register, and the evidence ledger are pinned to a specific commit in a public repository. That matters because the project did not fix the problem by making the model smarter. It fixed the problem by making the errors findable, disputable, and removable before they could harden into claims that no one could trace back to their evidence. The model did not need a better personality. The workflow needed stronger rules about which artifact was allowed to settle a claim.

What the project had surfaced, without naming it yet, was a pattern that extends beyond one pilot.

A system incurs confidence debt when its output is trusted downstream before the evidence warrants that trust. The term parallels technical debt, which borrows development velocity against future maintenance cost, and epistemic debt, which borrows assisted output against retained understanding. Confidence debt borrows trust itself. The output looks authoritative enough to propagate, but the evidence behind it has not been adjudicated to the standard that the downstream consumer will assume. The cost arrives not when the output is generated but when someone needs to dispute, reverse, or repair a claim that was never properly examined. By that point the claim has already traveled. It has been forwarded, embedded in decisions, or used as the basis for further work. The distance between how far trust propagated and how far the evidence actually reached defines the size of the debt.

And the debt compounds, because each downstream consumer inherits the upstream confidence without seeing what was compressed away. That compounding makes the pattern dangerous in a way that single-point fixes cannot address. It operates across the layers of the delegation stack: the artifacts a system produces, the evaluation scores those artifacts earn, and the human capacity to catch what went wrong once those scores justify deployment.

The evaluation layer is where confidence debt gets formally packaged and exported.

A model that solves retail support tasks 61 percent of the time sounds close to useful. In `tau-bench`, that same result changes shape once you ask what happens over repeated runs. The best model reaches 61 percent on individual attempts, but the pass-to-the-eighth falls below 25 percent. The model did not lose capability between the first run and the eighth. What changed was the question. The topline score answered whether the model could do the task. The repeated-run result started answering whether the model could be trusted to do it reliably. Those are different questions carrying different operational weight, but the headline flattens them into one number.

The field has improved benchmark realism faster than it has improved benchmark interpretation. Modern agent evaluations already rebuild real websites, localize failures, inject noise, and force agents to search large toolspaces. `WebSuite` raises the engineering value of evaluation by making failures attributable: a flat web score tells you less than learning that the agent breaks specifically on complex forms or ambiguous link selection. `WebForge` raises the environmental bar through noise injection and multidimensional difficulty control. Read together, these papers are not making leaderboards more impressive. They are making topline scores less sufficient as summaries of what the benchmark actually found.

The reliability literature explains why that insufficiency compounds. Consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety are separable dimensions that a single capability score cannot represent. A system can score well on task completion while trailing badly on any of them. The central pattern is the one the evaluation community keeps rediscovering: accuracy rises while reliability lags behind. Better capability numbers can therefore coexist with weak operational trust, and the gap between the two is precisely where confidence debt gets incurred at the evaluation layer. A pass rate becomes a headline. The headline enters a deployment argument. The deployment argument reaches a procurement meeting, a product roadmap, or a policy framework in a form that no longer carries the caveats the benchmark itself revealed. The score has traveled further than the evidence behind it. The debt is now portable.

The consequence of that debt landing at the human layer is different from the consequence at the artifact or evaluation layer. At those earlier layers, the cost is misjudgment. At the human layer, the cost is the quiet erosion of the capacity to catch misjudgments in the future.

In a set of randomized trials, people spent roughly ten minutes using AI assistance on straightforward problems. While the assistant was available, performance looked fine. Once it was removed, the same people solved fewer problems and gave up more often. The system had not failed in the usual sense. It had helped. The damage appeared in the residue it left behind: reduced persistence, weaker independent performance, and a measurable increase in surrendering tasks. Persistence matters because it sits upstream of correction. A person who stops working through a problem stops building the judgment that later oversight will need. The erosion is not about knowing less. It is about exercising less of the capacity that intervention requires.

The mechanism becomes clearer once interaction pattern enters the picture. In a study of developers learning a new asynchronous Python library, AI assistance reduced conceptual understanding, code reading ability, and debugging performance. The meaningful distinction was not AI versus no AI. It was how the person engaged with the AI. Patterns that forced the developer to ask for explanations, work with concepts, and stay cognitively active preserved learning. Patterns that cast the model as a contractor and the developer as a reviewer of finished work produced the steepest skill erosion. The pedagogical version of this is epistemic debt: in a study of novice programmers, unrestricted AI assistance and scaffolded AI produced similar short-term output, but they diverged sharply in a subsequent maintenance task once the assistant was removed. Some kinds of friction were carrying competence-preserving load that the workflow had quietly stopped accounting for.

The safety-engineering literature sharpens the compounding risk. The competence shadow describes a structural dynamic, not just a metaphor. When AI narrows the set of hazards, scenarios, or mitigations that get considered, downstream human review only inspects what survived that initial narrowing. The omission compounds. What disappears at the proposal stage cannot be recovered by careful inspection later, because it never reaches inspection. If the system proposes the initial search space, the human evaluates that reduced space, and a later review checks the outputs of that already-reduced process, the losses do not add up. They stack. The human is no longer correcting the whole problem space. They are correcting inside a machine-pruned version of it, and they may never see the boundary of what was pruned.

Each layer described so far is a recognized problem within its own community. Confident AI artifacts are a known hazard in documentation and verification. Over-read benchmark scores are an active topic in the evaluation literature. Human skill erosion under AI assistance is a growing concern across education, medicine, and software engineering. The reason they are not three independent problems is that they compose a pipeline. Confident artifacts generate the debt. Evaluation scores formalize and export it. Deployed assistance degrades the human correction capacity that was supposed to catch the errors the evaluation failed to measure. Each layer's confidence debt becomes the next layer's inherited premise.

The verification project illustrates the full cycle within a single case. A model produced a confident summary. No independent evaluation framework adjudicated the summary against the raw evidence. A reviewer would have consumed the polished output rather than tracing every mapping to source code, because that is what polished output is designed to encourage. If the project had not invented adjudication rules after the fact, the confident version would have propagated as proof. That is one project producing all three layers of the cascade: artifact confidence that outran the evidence, an evaluation gap where no benchmark challenged the confidence, and a human-layer exposure where the reviewer's capacity to catch the error was pre-empted by the plausible output.

At scale, the compounding follows the same structure. Over-read evaluation scores justify deployment decisions. Deployed systems produce confident outputs that move downstream before the evidence catches up. The operators who are supposed to supervise those outputs have had their own correction capacity attenuated by the same kind of AI assistance the pipeline now asks them to oversee. Within-layer fixes do not address this cascade. Better model calibration at the artifact layer does not prevent an aggregate score from being over-read at the evaluation layer. Better uncertainty indicators at the evaluation layer do not restore correction capacity that was never exercised at the human layer. Better interface friction at the human layer does not fix the problem if the evidence that reaches the human was already over-compressed before it arrived.

The strongest rival frame remains appropriate reliance. Calibration matters. Under-reliance and over-reliance are both real and both documented. In prostate cancer MRI diagnosis, radiologists under-relied on a stronger AI system, and targeted performance feedback did not straightforwardly solve the problem. In computational pathology, researchers can measure automation bias, anchoring effects, confirmation bias, and their amplification under time pressure. Those findings are important. They demonstrate that the reliance problem has more than one direction and more than one cause. The reason they do not absorb the deeper pattern is that they operate at the behavioral surface. They describe how people weight machine advice at the moment of decision. Confidence debt asks a prior question: what capacity remains available to weight the advice well, and has the workflow already compromised that capacity before the reliance decision even occurs? A more constructive alternative appears in hypothesis-driven decision support, which presents evidence for and against a candidate judgment rather than simply issuing a recommendation. The distinction is structural: recommendation-driven systems organize the human around reacting to the machine's answer, while hypothesis-driven systems preserve comparison, visibility into reasons, and room to reject the machine without abandoning the task.

The discipline that matters is specific enough to audit. At the artifact layer: establish evidence hierarchies, track contradictions as first-class objects, and make the wrong answers findable before they harden into public claims. At the evaluation layer: read benchmark scores inside the boundary of what the evaluation design actually measured, and do not let a pass rate carry more operational confidence than the benchmark warrants. At the human layer: ask what the workflow preserves in the person, not only what it extracts from the model, and do not call a workflow safe to delegate through unless the humans in it retain enough independent judgment to detect, challenge, and repair errors when the system is wrong in ways that conceal their own wrongness.

Confidence debt accrues whenever a system's output moves downstream before the evidence catches up. It compounds because each layer of the delegation stack inherits the upstream confidence without seeing what was compressed away. And it comes due at the moment that matters most: when someone needs to push back against a conclusion the entire pipeline has already accepted, and discovers that the capacity to push back has been quietly spent.

## Limits

This essay does not claim that AI assistance always produces confidence debt or that every domain requires identical safeguards. It does not claim that the three-layer cascade has been causally demonstrated through a single controlled study. It claims something more specific: the structural conditions for cross-layer compounding exist, one worked case illustrates the full cycle, and converging evidence from evaluation science, behavioral research, and safety engineering supports the pattern from independent directions. The compounding thesis is a structural inference from that convergence, not a proven causal chain. It is enough to change how workflows are designed. It is not yet enough to certify a universal law.
