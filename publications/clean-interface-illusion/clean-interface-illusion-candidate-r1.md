# The Clean Interface Illusion

Benchmark headlines travel badly. A result begins life as a bounded measurement inside a particular environment, with a particular reward function, a particular evaluator, and a particular way of failing. By the time that result reaches a product roadmap, a launch post, or an investor conversation, it often sounds like something much larger. The model can do knowledge work. The agent can use tools. The system is ready for the real world. That inflation happens so often that the field barely notices it anymore. A score stops being a score and becomes a trust signal.

That habit made sense when agent benchmarks were still easy to dismiss as toy settings. It makes less sense now. The strongest recent work has spent the last two years attacking the exact weaknesses critics kept naming. *WebSuite* was built because binary end-to-end results told us too little about why web agents fail. *REAL* rebuilt real sites into deterministic environments so realism did not have to die in order for reproducibility to live. *tau-bench* moved agent evaluation into policy-laden, multi-turn interaction. *ToolMisuseBench* added replayable faults, interface drift, authorization failures, and recovery budgets. *WebForge* went even further and argued that browser benchmarking had been trapped inside a realism, reproducibility, and scalability trilemma, then proposed an automated pipeline for escaping it. The benchmark builders are not asleep. They know the easy critique.

That is why the live dispute has changed. The hardest problem is no longer that agent evaluations are unreal. The harder problem is that benchmark interpretation is still unreal. Point success rates, even on much richer benchmarks, are still read as if they measured deployment readiness in the large. They do not. They often measure something narrower and still valuable: task completion inside a carefully defined environment. The trouble begins when that narrow signal is treated as operational trust.

Operational trust is a stricter object than benchmark success. It asks whether the system behaves within predictable bounds when the same task is repeated, when the environment shifts, when the tool schema drifts, when the objective is only partly stated, when the reward is not the whole story, and when a mistake carries a permission boundary with it. It asks whether the agent recovers, not just whether it occasionally completes. It asks whether failure is partitionable or erratic. It asks whether the system knows enough to hesitate. A benchmark score can contribute to that judgment. It cannot stand in for it.

The recent reliability literature states the gap plainly. *Towards a Science of AI Agent Reliability* argues that single-metric evaluation obscures the behavioral properties that matter most in deployment. Its four-way decomposition into consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety is useful not because it replaces capability measurement, but because it explains why capability alone keeps disappointing people. The paper’s central figure is hard to ignore: accuracy rises steadily while reliability trails behind. That is not a minor bookkeeping issue. It means the field can keep publishing better-looking results while the operational meaning of those results remains unstable.

The distinction matters because a system that reaches a respectable average score can still be a poor deployment object in two very different ways. One system may fail on a stable, recognizable subset of tasks. That kind of weakness is serious, but it can often be routed, bounded, or escalated around. Another system may fail unpredictably across repeated runs, perturbations, or hidden state changes. The average score can look similar while the operational meaning is entirely different. The former invites scope control. The latter poisons planning.

This is where the newer benchmarks help, and where they are still easy to misread. *WebSuite* is valuable because it treats failure attribution as a first-class object. In its introduction, the authors describe why existing benchmarks were inadequate: we could see that agents were weak, but not why. Their answer was not to chase a more dramatic aggregate number. It was to break web interaction into a taxonomy that makes failure localizable. That move is more important than it first appears. Once a benchmark can say that the agent breaks on complex form filling, or link selection inside a search result list, or some other repeatable interaction pattern, the score starts to acquire engineering meaning. A flat pass rate alone never had that meaning.

The same lesson appears in *TheAgentCompany*, though from a different angle. The paper does not argue that agents are worthless. It says something more sobering and more informative. In a simulated workplace built on self-hostable systems, the best model autonomously completed 30.3% of the tasks and reached 39.3% on a partial-credit metric. That is enough to prove that these systems are already useful in bounded settings. It is nowhere near enough to justify the fantasy that a general office agent is about to become a drop-in labor substitute. The paper becomes more interesting when you refuse to squeeze it into a single sentiment. The result is neither triumph nor failure. It is a boundary line.

The clean-interface critique from *From Task Solving to Robust Real-World Adaptation in LLM Agents* sharpens that boundary. The abstract is unusually direct. Many evaluations, the authors argue, assume a clean interface where dynamics are specified and stable, tools and sensors are reliable, and success is captured by a single explicit objective. Real deployment is messier: underspecified rules, unreliable signals, shifting environments, and implicit multi-stakeholder goals. That framing is useful, but only up to a point. If we stop there, we end up repeating yesterday’s criticism. The better reading is that the paper names the operational variables that a benchmark score must be indexed to before it can be trusted. Once those variables are explicit, the problem is not simply that existing benchmarks missed them. The problem is that people still read scores as though those variables did not exist.

That misreading becomes more obvious in the benchmarks that already include the missing stressors. *REAL* rebuilt real websites into deterministic replicas and still found that no frontier agent cleared 41.07% success. *tau-bench* put agents into multi-turn customer-service settings with domain policies and saw the best model reach 61% in retail and 35% in airline, while repeated-trial reliability collapsed fast enough that pass-to-the-eighth fell below 25% in retail. Those are not clean environments in the old sense. They include ambiguity, policy pressure, hidden state, and interaction drift. Yet even there, the temptation remains to treat the top-line result as the whole story. The real lesson is narrower. These systems can perform nontrivial work, but repeated, perturbation-sensitive reliability remains far shakier than the score alone suggests.

The tool-use literature pushes the same point from another direction. *LiveMCPBench* matters because it forces agents into a large MCP ecosystem where the problem is not merely calling a known function, but locating the right tool inside a noisy, distributed surface and then composing multiple steps across it. That benchmark is not hiding the hard part. It is built around the hard part. The paper’s argument, and the surrounding MCP benchmark line, show that once toolspaces become large, retrieval discipline and composition strategy start to dominate outcomes. A score earned there is already more realistic than the earlier generation of tool-use demos. Even so, it remains easy to overread. A model that performs well in such a setting may be good at retrieving and sequencing tools under that benchmark’s assumptions while still being weak at recovery, calibration, or permission-sensitive restraint.

This is also where the stakes stop being methodological and start becoming organizational. Security-oriented agent benchmarks such as *MCPTox* and *GrantBox* are useful not because they prove every deployed agent is catastrophically unsafe, but because they show how costly interpretive inflation becomes once tool use carries privileges. If a benchmark result is read as a general readiness signal, people will grant access on the basis of the wrong object. They will treat a task score as evidence of trustworthy behavior under privilege, even though those are different claims. The danger is not that one metric is mathematically flawed. The danger is that one metric is being asked to authorize a decision it was never designed to authorize.

This is why *WebForge* is a decisive paper for the essay rather than an inconvenience to it. On the surface it looks like a rebuttal. If browser benchmarks now have automated generation, self-contained environments, injected noise, and seven dimensions of difficulty control, then perhaps the realism complaint is finished and the field can get back to celebrating the frontier. But *WebForge* actually helps reveal the deeper problem. Its whole contribution depends on the claim that multi-dimensional evaluation reveals capability profiles invisible to a single aggregate score. That point does not weaken the essay. It is the essay. The benchmark surface got richer. The reading discipline around benchmark outputs did not improve at the same speed.

That leaves an obvious objection. If the community already knows how to build richer evaluations, and several papers explicitly warn against naive interpretation, then perhaps there is no real dispute here. Perhaps the field already corrected itself. I do not think that is true. The research literature is getting more careful faster than the ambient discourse around it. Benchmark builders now distinguish task validity from outcome validity, or capability from reliability, or aggregate completion from failure attribution. Those distinctions are not yet how results are usually discussed once they leave the paper. A result still gets narrated as a frontier capability win long before anyone asks what kind of operational confidence it actually licenses.

The doctrine that follows is simple enough to travel. A benchmark score should license only the confidence its design actually measured. If the benchmark measured task completion under a bounded environment, then the score licenses bounded-environment task completion. If it measured repeated-run stability, then it says something about consistency. If it measured perturbation resistance, then it says something about robustness under that perturbation family. If it measured tool retrieval at scale, then it says something about retrieval discipline in a large toolspace. Every step beyond that is narrative inflation.

That may sound austere. It is actually a better path to progress. Once the field stops asking one number to stand in for operational trust, benchmark work becomes more useful rather than less. A score can be joined to a reliability profile. A capability result can be paired with an uncertainty statement. A browser benchmark can reveal which failures are localizable interaction bugs and which are deeper planning instabilities. A tool benchmark can separate schema compliance from permission discipline. A deployment review can ask whether a measured success rate is supported by consistency and safety evidence or only by average completion. Those are not ceremonial refinements. They are the difference between evaluation as publicity and evaluation as governance.

The clean interface illusion survives because it was never only about interfaces. It was always about what gets prepaid by the measurement and what gets imagined afterward. The benchmark designers are already stripping away more of that prepaid structure. The next correction has to happen on the reading side. A score is not a system. A pass rate is not a delegation policy. And a leaderboard result, no matter how realistic the benchmark beneath it has become, is still not permission to pretend that operational trust has already been earned.

## Limits

- This draft does not claim that modern agent benchmarks are still trapped in toy settings.
- This draft does not claim that aggregate scores are useless. It claims they are routinely overread.
- This draft does not claim that current agents have no practical value. Several of the cited benchmarks show bounded but real usefulness.
- This draft does not try to settle procurement policy or deployment authorization in regulated domains. It is making an editorial and methodological claim about what benchmark results should license.
