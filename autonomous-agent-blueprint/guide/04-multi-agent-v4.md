# Operating Agents IV: One Agent Is Not Enough

The public story about multi-agent systems is still badly distorted by the way the term is used. It is often presented as a raw capability upgrade, as though several agents naturally imply more intelligence, more creativity, or more autonomy than one. The practical literature says something stricter. A multi-agent system is not one idea. It is an architectural decision to buy a boundary that a single-agent workflow could not provide cleanly. Sometimes that boundary is about permissions. Sometimes it is about critique. Sometimes it is about moving work through typed artifacts and distinct local contexts. Those are different problems, and they lead to different systems. Most disappointing multi-agent workflows are built by confusing them.

This is why the blueprint's three-way split is so important. Role separation is one problem. Reliability through critique is another. Task decomposition through artifact handoffs is a third. If a team says it is building a multi-agent system but cannot say which of those problems the second agent is solving, it is usually building theater. The system may still be elaborate. It may still produce long transcripts full of specialized roles. But added narration is not added architecture. The extra role only matters if it changes authority, evidence flow, or review.

Role separation is the cleanest reason to add another agent because it creates a real boundary. An executor should not necessarily hold the same permissions as a reviewer. A planner may need broad read access but no write authority. A compliance or approval role may need independence precisely because accountability is the point. In those cases the second agent is not there to sound different. It is there to make the system's authority structure explicit. That is a genuine engineering reason to split. The split narrows blast radius, clarifies who was allowed to do what, and can make audits much easier to run after a failure.

Critique is a different justification and a much easier one to misuse. Debate systems and reviewer overlays can help when the first pass suffers from predictable blind spots, when the cost of a bad output is high enough to justify another view, or when disagreement itself surfaces useful error signals. Papers like MAD and RECONCILE matter because they show that multiple agents can improve reasoning quality when the task truly benefits from structured disagreement and reconciliation. But the fact that critique can help does not mean it should become the default answer to weak performance. Many teams deploy reviewer agents where a deterministic verification pass would have been cheaper, faster, and easier to govern. Critique is real. It is just narrower than the field's marketing around multi-agent systems suggests (Liang et al.; Chen et al.).

Task decomposition through artifacts is the third reason, and in many practical workflows it is the most underappreciated one. Some tasks really do have stages that deserve different local contexts and different deliverables. A planner writes a spec. A retriever assembles an evidence packet. An executor produces an artifact against that evidence. A reviewer checks the result against explicit criteria. The key point is not that different agents are talking. The key point is that different stages are exchanging typed objects. MetaGPT and ChatDev matter because they push multi-agent design toward that artifact-bearing model rather than toward one sprawling conversation. The moment the workflow is organized around plans, drafts, patches, evidence packets, or pass-fail reports, handoffs become legible and failure localization improves sharply (Hong et al.; Qian et al.).

This is also why conversation-centric frameworks need to be read with care. AutoGen made multi-agent conversation much easier to program, which is a real contribution. It lowered the barrier to experimenting with agent interaction patterns, human-in-the-loop turns, and tool-augmented roles. But that flexibility is also why the framework is such a useful cautionary reference. When conversation becomes the primary fabric of coordination, it becomes easy to let free-form exchange substitute for interface design. One role passes a long message that mixes instructions, evidence, assumptions, and tentative conclusions. The next role has to infer what is binding, what is noise, and what counts as the task state. By the time the system fails, nobody can localize where the failure entered because the boundary between stages was never explicit enough to inspect (Wu et al.).

The explanatory move the blueprint only hints at is that multi-agent design is really about how complexity is purchased. A second agent does not create free intelligence. It creates another handoff, another state boundary, another evaluation point, and another place where ambiguity can either be reduced or multiplied. That means every extra agent imposes coordination debt. If the handoff is typed and the authority boundary is real, the debt may be worth paying. If the handoff is conversational and the roles are mostly decorative, the system becomes slower, harder to audit, and harder to fix. This is why the chapter's best practical rule is so severe: the split has to buy a boundary.

The human role becomes clearer once that rule is taken seriously. Many decisions that get described as multi-agent questions are really questions about where the human sits. Where is approval mandatory? Which outputs can proceed automatically and which ones require review? What evidence must be attached before a downstream role can act? When does escalation become necessary? These decisions often matter more than whether the system uses two model roles or five. A planner plus reviewer plus human approval gate is frequently a stronger architecture than a seven-agent committee because it has a reason to exist, a visible handoff chain, and a place where accountability actually lands.

This perspective also explains why protocol talk is secondary to workflow design. Inter-agent message standards and agent-to-agent infrastructure may become more important over time, but they do not rescue a topology that has no reason to exist. A bad split with a cleaner protocol is still a bad split. A workflow that cannot explain why roles are separate, what artifact they exchange, how their outputs are evaluated, and where a human can reconstruct failure does not become sound by adding more infrastructure. The architecture has to come first. Protocols help after the boundary is justified, not before.

So the practical doctrine of the chapter is conservative for good reason. Start with one agent if one agent can own the task with bounded tools and a clear control loop. Split only when role separation is real, when critique has an identifiable reliability benefit, or when the work naturally moves through explicit artifacts that deserve separate contexts. Prefer typed handoffs over free-form chat. Add reviewer or debate roles only when the task is valuable enough to justify their cost and only when their outputs can be evaluated meaningfully. Keep the human in the architecture wherever approval, policy judgment, or accountability require it. That is not anti-multi-agent. It is anti-confusion.

## Resources And References

### [Builder Blueprint: Multi-Agent Design](https://github.com/petroslamb/content/blob/main/autonomous-agent-blueprint/practical-chapter-blueprints/05-multi-agent-design-blueprint.md)

This blueprint matters because it turns the essay's three-way split into concrete guidance for when to separate roles, when to add critique, and when typed artifact handoffs justify another agent.

### [MetaGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.00352) (2024)

MetaGPT matters because it gives the strongest practical argument for multi-agent systems that are organized around artifacts rather than around conversational improvisation. Its core contribution is not merely that it names multiple roles. It is that those roles exchange structured outputs within a workflow that resembles an engineered production process. That makes the system much more useful as an architecture reference than looser "agent society" examples (Hong et al.).

It is also a useful warning. MetaGPT is easy to imitate superficially. A team can copy role names and still miss the actual point, which is that the roles should exist because they carry different responsibilities, contexts, and deliverables. The paper matters precisely because it makes that distinction harder to ignore.

### [ChatDev](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.07924) (2023)

ChatDev matters because it shows how role-based collaboration can be made legible within a domain that naturally produces artifacts. Software work, like some research and documentation work, already has stages that generate drafts, code, tests, and revisions. The paper is therefore valuable not because every team should simulate a software company, but because it demonstrates how staged outputs can constrain coordination in a useful way (Qian et al.).

Its limitation is domain shape. A workflow that copies the visible form of ChatDev without sharing the same artifact-bearing structure will likely drift into role theater. That is why the paper is most useful when treated as a model for explicit deliverables and less useful when treated as a license to multiply personas.

### [AutoGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08155) (2023)

AutoGen matters because it normalized programmable multi-agent conversation as a practical design surface. It made it easier to combine agents, humans, and tools in flexible message loops. That contribution is real. It opened a path for rapid experimentation and helped demonstrate how many coordination patterns can be built once the conversation layer becomes configurable (Wu et al.).

At the same time, it is one of the best cautionary papers in the chapter because its strength is also its danger. Flexible conversation is not the same thing as sound decomposition. When stopping conditions, artifact expectations, and authority boundaries are vague, the conversation fabric becomes a place where ambiguity compounds rather than a place where work is clarified.

### [Encouraging Divergent Thinking in Large Language Models through Multi-Agent Debate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19118) (2024) and [RECONCILE: Round-Table Conference Improves Reasoning via Consensus among Diverse LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.13007) (2023)

MAD and RECONCILE matter because they make disagreement and consensus concrete enough to evaluate. They are valuable not as general proofs that many voices are always better, but as demonstrations that reliability can improve when alternative views are structured, challenged, and reconciled deliberately (Liang et al.; Chen et al.).

Their lesson is narrower than the broader multi-agent hype suggests. Debate and consensus are expensive overlays. They belong where error reduction is worth the cost and where disagreement actually carries signal. Used casually, they become latency with good branding. Used carefully, they show when a second view genuinely improves judgment.
