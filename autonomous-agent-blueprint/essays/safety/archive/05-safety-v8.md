# Operating Agents V: The Attack Surface You Built

An internal operations workflow could read support tickets, search documentation, draft internal tasks, and escalate payment issues into a billing system. The team behind it was not careless. They had written strong instructions, told the model to be cautious with untrusted content, and added monitoring once the workflow was live. For a while that looked like enough, partly because nothing dramatic happened. Then a crafted support article entered the system through an ordinary retrieval path. It looked harmless on a quick read: copied troubleshooting text, a routine note about escalation, and one line suggesting that attaching account details would speed manual handling. The workflow read the ticket, retrieved the article, drafted an internal escalation, and passed that escalation forward as though it were trusted internal work. By the time analysts noticed, they were cleaning up artifacts that looked normal precisely because they had been generated inside the workflow rather than injected from the outside.

What failed there was not some exotic property of the model. The workflow had simply collapsed several different trust categories into one stream. It treated retrieved content as though it were close enough to system guidance to shape the next step. It treated an internally generated artifact as though its internal origin had already earned it authority. It let a content channel acquire reach that no one had named explicitly. That is the safety problem that matters in modern agent systems. Once a workflow can read, remember, hand off, and act, safety stops being mainly a question about whether the prompt sounds careful enough. It becomes a question about which channels exist, what each channel is allowed to influence, and where trust is supposed to stop.

## Safety Begins At The Boundary Between Data And Authority

The simplest way to misunderstand agent safety is to inherit the old chatbot intuition and carry it into a system that now has tools, memory, and workflow state. In that older frame, the main concern is usually the model producing an unsafe answer. In an agent workflow the more consequential question is often earlier and more architectural. What enters the system as data. What enters it as instruction. What survives as memory. What can cross a handoff. What can trigger a tool. What can move from plausible text into external consequence. The danger is not only that hostile content exists. The danger is that the architecture may quietly permit hostile or low-trust content to travel farther than anyone intended.

That is why the first serious safety question is deliberately dull. What does this channel reach. If a retrieved paragraph can influence planning, then retrieval is part of the safety story. If a memory write can shape later runs, then memory policy is part of the safety story. If one component can hand another a structured artifact that changes what happens next, then handoff design is part of the safety story. Once those questions are stated plainly, much of the prompt-centered safety conversation starts to look incomplete. A careful prompt can help with local behavior, but it does not by itself establish trust classes, narrow tool scope, validate handoffs, retire poisoned memory, or insert approval before irreversible action.

The field needed a sharper way to say this, which is one reason *Not What You've Signed Up For* mattered so much. Its lasting contribution was not merely that it dramatized indirect prompt injection. It changed the trust model. Once a language system is integrated with external content and external tools, instructions and observations can no longer be treated as though they deserve the same authority. Retrieved passages are evidence, not privileged guidance. User text is intent and context, not policy. Another component's output is not safe simply because it was produced inside the same broader workflow. The paper made that shift vivid, but the architectural point is larger than the paper itself. If a system cannot preserve the distinction between content and authority, it is relying on hope more than control.

## Capability And Exposure Are The Same Architecture Seen From Different Sides

By this point in the series, a pattern should already be visible. The same mechanism that makes the system useful often creates the corresponding safety burden. Action surfaces create blast radius because a workflow that can send, write, delete, or submit can now turn the wrong inference into an external side effect. Memory creates persistence because a workflow that can retain useful state can also preserve poisoned state, stale state, or adversarially induced lessons across time. Multi-agent handoffs create propagation because the same pathways that let work move cleanly can also let contamination move cleanly. Retrieval creates exposure because the workflow now depends on material whose relevance may be high while its trustworthiness is low.

This is why it is misleading to treat safety as a later supplement to capability design. Capability design and safety design are often the same architecture viewed from different sides. A broad tool surface is both more reach and more blast radius. A richer memory layer is both more continuity and more persistence of error. A looser handoff layer is both more flexibility and more room for trust to leak across boundaries. Once that symmetry becomes clear, the right design instinct becomes less theatrical. The task is not to make the model sound vigilant in every situation. The task is to make the interfaces explicit enough that the wrong content cannot quietly inherit the wrong authority.

That change in perspective is especially important because prompt-centric safety flatters the builder in exactly the wrong way. It makes the workflow look as though it is behaving safely because the model sounds careful, refuses suspicious requests, or repeats the right policy phrases. In deployed systems, that kind of surface caution is useful but secondary. The primary question is structural. If the workflow is mistaken, manipulated, or simply confused, what can it still reach. If the answer is broad tools, durable memory, permissive handoffs, and externally visible action, then a careful tone is not a serious control.

## Retrieval And Memory Are Part Of The Integrity Model

Modern agent writing still too often treats retrieval and memory as quality layers first and security layers second. That ordering breaks down the moment the workflow acts on what it retrieves or what it previously stored. A knowledge store is not neutral substrate once retrieved material can shape planning, classification, routing, or external action. It becomes part of the integrity model of the system. *PoisonedRAG* mattered because it made this point difficult to dismiss. A relatively small amount of poisoned content can materially distort what the workflow sees and therefore what it is likely to do. The deeper lesson is not simply that malicious documents can exist in a corpus. It is that retrieval itself is an influence surface, and influence surfaces need governance.

The same logic extends to memory. A workflow that writes summaries, facts, lessons, or preferences into durable state is creating future influence channels. A poisoned memory write is not one bad answer in isolation. It is one bad answer that now has a path into later decisions. That is why provenance, freshness, versioning, controlled write policy, anomaly review, and correction paths belong inside the same design conversation rather than being treated as secondary hygiene tasks. Once a system can write casually into a store and later trust that same store without any intervening control surface, it has granted itself a feedback path that can amplify contamination across time.

The strongest safety posture therefore treats retrieval sources and memory stores as governed infrastructure rather than convenience layers. The right question is not only whether the system retrieved something relevant. It is whether the system retrieved something that still deserved authority in the present run, and whether the run had enough structure to distinguish evidence from instruction while doing so.

## Handoffs Need Skepticism, Not Sentiment

One of the quietest mistakes in agent workflows is to treat internal artifacts as trustworthy simply because they were produced internally. This is easy to do because internal outputs often carry the rhetorical shape of sanctioned state. They arrive formatted, contextualized, and wrapped in the language of the workflow. A summary written by one component can therefore smuggle a poisoned instruction. A screenshot can carry hostile content into a later review stage. A structured packet can freeze stale evidence into a form that looks more authoritative than the underlying material ever was.

That is why trust has to reset at the handoff rather than glide through it. The receiving component still has to ask what evidence supports the object, what the object is allowed to influence, and what should remain outside its authority. Typed handoffs help because they reduce ambiguity and make the contract inspectable. They do not eliminate the need for skepticism. Another agent's output is not a system instruction merely because it came from another agent. Internal origin may justify different handling. It does not justify automatic trust.

This is one reason papers such as *Agent Smith* are useful beyond their most dramatic setup. The multimodal propagation story is vivid, but the broader point is architectural. Once artifacts move through the same channels legitimate coordination uses, contamination can move with them unless the handoff layer was designed to distinguish form from authority.

## Tool Scope And Approval Decide Whether A Mistake Stays Local

The same architecture shows up on the action side. Once a workflow can produce side effects, tool scope becomes a core safety decision. A tool that searches documentation does not belong in the same category as a tool that writes to a customer record. A tool that drafts an internal note does not belong in the same category as one that can send an external message or execute a destructive change. Least privilege matters here not as a moral slogan but as a mechanical way of limiting what a compromised or misled workflow can do before somebody notices.

Approval belongs in the same family of controls. Human approval is often discussed as though it were a residue left over from less capable systems. In serious workflows it is usually better understood as an accountability boundary. Financial actions, destructive writes, external commitments, legal or policy-sensitive moves, and other hard-to-reverse operations should not rely on prompt tone for protection. They need scoped tools, deterministic checks, and a visible point at which responsibility is owned. A safety posture that cannot say where approval sits is not finished, because it cannot yet say where consequence becomes answerable to review.

## Safety Work Should Arrive Before The Damage Teaches It

The last major mistake is chronological. Teams often postpone safety because the capability layer still feels unstable. They plan to narrow permissions later, curate the corpus later, formalize the handoffs later, and add approval once the workflow proves its value. That sequence is convenient during early iteration and expensive once the workflow has begun to depend on its most permissive form. Broad early permissions become hidden dependencies. Casual memory writes become expensive cleanup problems. Loose handoffs become harder to formalize because downstream stages have learned to rely on their looseness.

This is why *ToolEmu* matters as more than a sandboxing paper. Its most useful contribution is sequencing. Pressure-test the workflow before widening autonomy. Run abuse cases against the actual channels the architecture exposes. Feed the retriever poisoned but plausible documents. Insert misleading instructions into help text. Simulate stale internal artifacts that look sanctioned. Test what happens when one component hands another a structured object with the wrong evidence attached. Then ask what the workflow allowed that content to reach, what policy interrupted it, and whether the logs would let a reviewer reconstruct what happened afterward.

That kind of rehearsal does not replace containment. It reveals where containment is thin. A safe workflow still gets pressured. The question is whether pressure can move across channels without leaving a trail or colliding with an explicit boundary.

The conservative doctrine that follows from all this is plain enough to sound obvious and expensive enough to ignore at your peril. Separate instructions from data. Treat retrieval and memory as integrity-sensitive infrastructure. Scope tools by least privilege. Reset trust at handoffs. Put deterministic checks and human approval in front of consequential action. Stress the workflow before expanding its freedom. More autonomy is not a neutral feature release. It is a wider attack surface. If that surface grows faster than the boundaries around it, the architecture is being asked to trust what it has not yet learned how to govern.

## Selected Sources

[Not What You've Signed Up For](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.12173) matters because it forced the field to separate instructions from data in integrated systems where external content can influence planning and action.

[PoisonedRAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.07867) matters because it made retrieval integrity impossible to treat as a side issue. Once retrieved content can guide behavior, the corpus becomes part of the system's safety model.

[ToolEmu](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.15817) matters because it moved adversarial evaluation in front of deployment rather than after consequence.

[Agent Smith](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.08567) matters because it dramatizes a broader truth about propagation across artifacts and roles: legitimate coordination channels can also carry contamination.

[BadRAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.00083) and [Machine Against the RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.05870) matter because they widen the retrieval threat model beyond one narrow poisoning story and reinforce the distinction between relevance and trust.
