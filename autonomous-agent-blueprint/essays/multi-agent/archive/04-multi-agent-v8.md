# Operating Agents IV: When a Second Agent Earns Its Keep

A team built a seven-role escalation workflow for customer exceptions: a planner, a researcher, a writer, a reviewer, an editor, a compliance checker, and a human approver. Each role had a careful description. Each handoff was a long conversational message. One role pasted policy excerpts, another rephrased them, another softened the recommendation, another announced that the result looked compliant. Six months in, an escalation went out on superseded policy and no one could localize where the stale clause had entered. The planner and researcher had the same permissions. The reviewer had no typed evidence packet to check. The human approver had to reconstruct the run from chat.

The system looked sophisticated. The split bought narration, not architecture.

That is the multi-agent trap. A second agent is not free intelligence. It is a boundary purchase, and the bill arrives as coordination debt.

The public story about multi-agent systems still treats them as though they sat on one simple axis of more capability. More roles, more perspectives, more intelligence. The practical literature says something stricter. A multi-agent system is not one upgrade. It is a choice to buy a boundary that a single-agent workflow could not provide cleanly. That boundary can be worth a great deal. It can also cost more than it returns. Every extra role adds another handoff, another local context, another evaluation burden, and another place where ambiguity can either be reduced or multiplied. If the split does not change authority, evidence flow, or review structure, the system gets more expensive without becoming more legible.

That is why the default should stay severe. Start with one agent, bounded tools, and a clear control loop. Add another role only when the split buys something a single-agent workflow cannot buy as cheaply. In practice, there are only a few reasons that count, and they are easy to name once the rhetoric is stripped away.

The first reason is permission. Sometimes the system should not let one component both decide and act with the same authority. An executor should not necessarily share the same permissions as a reviewer. A planner may need broad read access but no write authority. A compliance or approval role may need genuine independence because accountability is the point. This is the cleanest reason to split roles because it changes what each role is allowed to do. The second role is not there to sound different. It is there to make the authority structure explicit and narrower. The human role often matters most here. In serious workflows, a human approver is not a leftover from a less advanced system. It is often the boundary the rest of the design is trying to protect.

The second reason is independent critique. Some workflows do not fail because one role had too much authority. They fail because the first pass has predictable blind spots and the cost of a miss is high enough to justify another view. This is where debate and reviewer patterns earn their keep. The useful move is not many voices are better. The useful move is that a second role can challenge an evidence gap, a reasoning shortcut, or a weak recommendation when the task truly benefits from an independent pass. That architecture is also easy to overuse. Many teams add reviewer agents where a deterministic verification stage would have been cheaper, faster, and easier to govern. Critique is real, but it is narrower than the marketing around multi-agent systems suggests. If the reviewer mostly paraphrases, if latency rises while quality barely moves, or if disagreement never changes the decision, the system is paying coordination debt for ceremony.

The third reason is typed artifact handoffs. Some tasks really do have stages that deserve different local contexts and different deliverables. A planner writes a spec. A retriever assembles an evidence packet. An executor produces an artifact against that packet. A reviewer checks the result against explicit criteria. The key point is not that several personalities are talking. The key point is that the stages exchange typed objects. *MetaGPT* mattered because it pushed multi-agent design back toward artifacts, documents, and staged outputs at a moment when the field was drifting toward role-play. *ChatDev* reinforced the same lesson in a more theatrical form. The useful part in both cases is not the role names. It is that a spec, an evidence packet, a patch, or a pass-fail record gives the next stage something inspectable to receive.

That is why handoff design is the real architecture of a multi-agent system. A long conversational message that mixes instructions, evidence, assumptions, and tentative conclusions forces the receiving role to reconstruct what is binding. Once the workflow fails, no one can say where the error entered because the handoff never became explicit enough to inspect. Typed handoffs do not solve every problem, but they change what failure looks like. They turn a conversation into a contract. That change matters more than the existence of several roles by itself.

The most practical way to judge whether a split was justified is to look at what happens after a miss. In a good topology, failure becomes easier to localize. The team can say which role had authority, which artifact carried the error, which review stage missed it, and whether the next role received the right object. In a bad topology, every extra role expands the search space for blame without improving the evidence. That is why the best single-agent baseline remains the right control case. If a multi-agent workflow cannot clearly beat one bounded agent with stronger tools, cleaner artifacts, and a sharper verification path, the extra topology is complexity without justification. Transcript sophistication is the wrong score. Failure localization is the right one.

This is also why *AutoGen* mattered in two different ways at once. It made programmable multi-agent conversation practical, which is a real contribution. It also made overbuilding easier. Once conversation becomes the default coordination fabric, it becomes easy to let flexible messaging substitute for interface design. Stopping conditions blur. Authority boundaries blur. Artifact expectations blur. The transcript grows more interesting while the workflow grows harder to govern. Protocol cleanliness alone cannot rescue a decorative split.

The conservative rule after deployment should therefore be just as strict as the rule before deployment. If a reviewer never changes the decision, collapse it. If a specialist has the same permissions, the same tools, and the same output contract as the orchestrator, collapse it. If a planning role produces prose that no downstream stage can check against a typed artifact, collapse it. Multi-agent systems become governable partly by being willing to get smaller. Role count is sticky once teams grow attached to the theater of specialization, but a named role is not the same thing as a load-bearing boundary.

The chapter's claim is therefore narrower than the multi-agent conversation usually is, and more useful for that reason. Extra agents multiply ambiguity unless they create a real boundary. Builders should act as though that sentence is expensive to violate, because it is.

## Selected Sources

[MAD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.19118) matters because it makes critique legible as a specific reliability intervention rather than as a general argument for more voices.

[MetaGPT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.00352) matters because it pushed multi-agent design back toward documents, artifacts, and staged outputs at a moment when the field was drifting toward role-play.

[ChatDev](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.07924) matters because it makes the artifact-pipeline lesson visible in a recognizable workflow and clarifies what staged decomposition can look like when the artifacts are inspectable.

[AutoGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08155) matters because it lowered the cost of building role-heavy systems and therefore lowered the cost of making decorative ones too.

[RECONCILE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.13007) matters because it sharpens the question of when disagreement carries real signal and when it is merely extra coordination overhead.
